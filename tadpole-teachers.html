Tadpole Trek classroom edition

Teacher guide

Tadpole Trek is a free 3D game from the SQuEACs Lab at Midwestern University. Students guide one tadpole from hatchling to froglet through a polluted pond. They learn that the time a tadpole spends in each kind of pollution changes how it develops. The game is built on the lab's research on the tadpole brain.

Learning goals by grade band

K–2

  • Living things need clean water and food.
  • Pollution can make a habitat unsafe.
  • People can help keep ponds clean.

Use: No predators, Younger readers, and Read to me. Play stages 1 and 2 as a class.

3–4

  • A tadpole changes into a froglet, so living things have life cycles.
  • Changes in a habitat affect the animals that live there.
  • Different pollutants cause different problems.

Use: Predictions before each stage and the reflection after it.

5–6

  • Longer exposure means a bigger effect.
  • Timing matters because the young brain is still forming.
  • Evidence, predictions, and models help scientists test ideas. Designs can reduce harm.

Use: The full trek, the Journal, the Anatomy window, and the Pond lab.

A 15-minute lesson

2 minAsk. "What do tadpoles need to grow? What could go wrong in a dirty pond?"
2 minPredict. Students choose Metals, PFAS, Litter, or Not sure on the mission screen.
6 minPlay. Students finish stage 1, or stages 1 and 2 with Quick play. Pairs share a device.
3 minReflect. Read the Think like a scientist box on the results screen and discuss the question.
2 minExit ticket. "One thing I learned about pollution and tadpoles is…"

Extension: 30 to 45 minutes

Play all five stages, then open Pond lab from the title screen. Students clean up six pollution sources and add their own to test ideas. Ask: "Which source would you clean up first, and why? What would stop new pollution from starting?"

Classroom settings

  • Predators off: relaxed swimming, best for K–2.
  • Younger readers: shorter, simpler science cards.
  • Read to me: your device reads each screen aloud in a low voice. It uses the voices on the device.
  • Big text: larger words in menus and cards.
  • Pattern labels: shapes and words on pollution clouds and the map, so color is not the only clue.
  • Quick play: about 40% less food to eat in each stage.

What is real and what is made up

The tadpole, the brain regions, and the idea that pollution can affect development come from real research. The numbers do not. Plume sizes, brain scores, and how fast meters fill are made up for play. The game presents effects as ideas being tested. Researchers still debate how PFAS affect tadpole brains, and the game's science page lists both sides. The full rules are on the How Tadpole Trek works page.

Tell students: "The game is a model. Real scientists measure real tadpoles to find out what really happens."

Privacy

  • No accounts and no student names. The game asks for nothing.
  • Progress saves only in the browser on that device. Students can erase it on the title screen.
  • The classroom edition does not send play counts to the lab.
  • The page loads its fonts from Google Fonts. The real 3D brain viewer loads from a public code host (unpkg) only when a student taps its button.

Check these details against your school or district rules for student use of web tools.
