How Tadpole Trek works

Tadpole Trek follows one tadpole from hatchling to froglet through a polluted pond. This page lays out exactly what the game does, so teachers, parents, and curious players can see the science behind it. The numbers are illustrative. They show how exposure could add up in a developing brain, not measurements from real tadpoles.

Play Tadpole Trek →

The idea in four lines

  • Exposure builds with time. Three meters (metals, PFAS, litter) fill while your tadpole swims inside a plume, and fill faster near the plume's center.
  • Metals and PFAS act on the brain. At the end of each stage, the meters shrink the scores of five brain regions.
  • Litter acts on food. Plastic and paper never touch the brain in the game. They remove algae and slow growth.
  • The brain feeds back into play. A smaller region makes the next stage harder in a specific way, so the damage you take is something you feel.

Five stages, one tadpole

The game uses five stages, named loosely after the standard Gosner scheme for staging frog embryos and larvae (Gosner, 1960) [verify]. The Gosner ranges are approximate, and the game does not model real developmental timing.

The brain sensitivity column is a made-up number that falls across development. It stands in for the idea that a brain may be most vulnerable while its regions form fastest. That idea, often called a critical window or sensitive period, is a hypothesis being tested, and the numbers are invented for the game.

The pond and its plumes

Every stage uses the same pond, 140 units long, with a new random layout of pollution. You start in the outfall plume at the west end and the clean habitat waits at the east end. Each stage places six plumes at random, plus one more per stage after the first, and always at least one litter plume on the straight route.

Outside its layer, a plume still counts at 25%, because pollutants spread. Inside a plume, exposure rises at a rate of 4.6 meter points per second times the plume's strength, which falls from 1 at the center to 0 at the edge (strength = (1 − distance ÷ radius)0.7). If plumes of the same meter overlap, the game uses the strongest one, not the sum. Meters stop at 100.

From exposure to brain change

When a stage ends, each brain region loses points:

Loss = sensitivity × (metal meter × metal weight + PFAS meter × PFAS weight) × 0.2

Scores start at 100, which stands for the clean-water value in the game, and never drop below 25. The weights below are invented. They are loosely inspired by two hypotheses from the Journal: that metals disturb forebrain and eye patterning, and that PFAS disturb the hormone signals that guide development.

The diencephalon appears in the drawings because it is on our 3D model, but the game does not score it. Two examples: a stage with both meters at 30 costs the telencephalon 12 points at sensitivity 1.0, and 6 points at sensitivity 0.5. A stage with both meters at 100 costs it 40 points at sensitivity 1.0.

If you end a stage without reaching the clean habitat, the game assumes you stayed in the polluted pond and adds 20 points to every meter.

Litter, food, and growth

  • Algae spawn away from litter. A spot inside a litter plume loses 85% of its algae. Inside metal and PFAS plumes, extra algae spawn on purpose, so the richest food is often the most toxic.
  • Growth per stage = 20 × (algae eaten ÷ target, held between 0.25 and 1) × (1 − 0.25 × litter meter ÷ 100).
  • Body size adds up across five stages to at most 100, the clean-water value.
  • Litter never changes a brain score. In real ponds, litter can do more, such as breaking into microplastics, and how much that matters for tadpoles is still being studied.

The final froglet

After stage 5, the game picks the first rule that matches, using averages across your five stages:

  • Clean Hopper: average brain score 85 or more and body size 85 or more.
  • Double Trouble: metal and PFAS meters both averaged 35 or more, and average brain score under 70.
  • Metal-marked or PFAS-marked: that meter averaged at least 30 and at least 10 above the other.
  • Hungry Hopper: body size under 60 and litter averaged 30 or more.
  • Slow Grower: body size under 65.
  • Survivor: everything else.

Predators and hearts

  • Dragonfly nymphs wait near plants and lunge when you pass within about 4.6 units, then rest. Swim away fast.
  • Fish patrol and chase at a speed just under yours. Hide in a plant cluster or outswim them.
  • Herons cast a shadow on the water for two seconds before striking the surface. Dive below 1.5 units deep to be safe.
  • You have three hearts. Losing all three restarts the stage, and your brain scores and size stay as they were. Rare bubble treasures restore a heart.
  • You can turn predators off for relaxed play. Exposure and brain effects work the same.

What the game simplifies

  • One tadpole gets one generation of exposure. The game does not model inheritance or evolution, and the effects are developmental, not heritable.
  • Real exposure depends on the specific chemical, its concentration, the species, and timing. Heavy metals and PFAS each include many different chemicals, and the game treats each as one thing.
  • Real effects on the brain are measured in many ways, such as shape, size, cell counts, gene activity, and behavior. The game boils them down to one size score per region.
  • Scientists do not yet agree on whether every effect in the game occurs in every species, and some studies of PFAS in amphibians report effects only at high concentrations or in other organs.
  • Plume sizes, weights, sensitivities, and all numbers on this page are illustrative.

Concepts and hypotheses in the Science Journal

The hypotheses below are ideas our lab and others are testing, not settled answers.

  • Bioindicators and sentinel species
  • Tadpole brain regions
  • Dose and duration of exposure
  • Critical windows and sensitive periods
  • Heavy metals and developmental neurotoxicity (teratogens; PAX6 and BMP4)
  • PFAS, thyroid disruption, and neurodevelopment
  • Indirect effects of litter on the food web
  • Mixture toxicity
  • Thyroid-hormone-driven metamorphosis
  • Lithobates heckscheri and our microCT model
