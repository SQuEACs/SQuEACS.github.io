Otter Evolution classroom edition

Teacher guide

Otter Evolution is a free 3D game from the SQuEACs Lab at Midwestern University. Students raise a population of 12 North American river otters (Lontra canadensis) for up to eight generations. They play one season at a time as a single otter, then watch natural selection, inheritance, mutation, and genetic drift change the whole population. The game is built on the lab's CT research on otter skulls, brains, and inner ears.

What is different in the classroom edition

  • Meet the river otter comes first. Before the first game, students read a short page about the real animal: where it lives, what it eats, how its body works in water, and why the lab studies it.
  • Science words are tappable. Key terms on the game screens (population, trait, natural selection, mutation, genetic drift, cochlea, and more) are underlined. Tapping one shows a short definition. A Key words list is on the title screen.
  • Predictions. Before each season, students can guess which trait will change most. After the pups are born, a Think like a scientist box compares the guess with what happened and asks a discussion question.
  • Short game plays 4 generations instead of 8, so a full run fits in one class.
  • Big text makes menus and cards larger.
  • Progress saves separately from the public game, and the classroom edition does not send play counts to the lab.

Learning goals by grade band

3–5

  • Animals of the same kind vary in their traits.
  • Young inherit traits from their parents.
  • In a given habitat, some animals survive and reproduce better than others because of their traits.
  • Body structures, like fur and the inner ear, help animals survive.

Use: Meet the river otter, a Short game, and the predictions.

6–8

  • Natural selection changes how common traits are in a population over generations.
  • Mutation adds new variation, and chance (genetic drift) can change a small population too.
  • Changes during one animal's life (plasticity) are not inherited.
  • Charts of population averages are evidence of evolution.

Use: All 8 generations, the Evolution chart, the final "Why each trait ended up where it did" cards, and a two-habitat class comparison.

A 15-minute lesson

2 minMeet the animal. Read Meet the river otter together. Ask: "What helps a river otter live in water? What would help an otter in a snowy place, or a hot desert?"
2 minPredict. Students pick a habitat, then choose which trait they think will change most on the mission screen.
7 minPlay. Students play two seasons. Pairs share a device: one drives, one records predictions and results on the data sheet, then they switch.
3 minReflect. Read the Think like a scientist box after the pups are born and discuss its question.
1 minExit ticket. "The population changed because…"

Extension: a two-habitat experiment (40 to 50 minutes)

Split the class. Half start in Snowy North, half in Desert River. Everyone plays a Short game or all 8 generations without moving. At the end, groups record the final averages from the results table on the board. Ask:

  • "Both groups started with the same kind of otters. Why did they end up different?"
  • "Which traits changed in opposite directions? Which barely changed? Why?"
  • "Did every group in the same habitat get exactly the same result? What made them differ?" (Luck, or genetic drift, and mutation.)

For a third group, try Murky Pond or Fast Mountain River and compare the cochlea and canal angle, which these habitats push.

Common misconceptions to listen for

Students may sayWhat the game shows
"My otter evolved thicker fur."Individuals don't evolve. The population changes because otters with suited traits leave more pups. Fur that thickens during one season is plasticity, and pups don't inherit it.
"The otters needed thicker fur, so they grew it."Mutations are random. Selection keeps the variation that happens to help. It can't make what is needed.
"Fittest means strongest."Fitness means surviving and having young. In the Desert River, smaller otters do better.
"The best-suited otter always survives."Luck matters. In a population of 12, a well-suited otter can be lost by chance. That is genetic drift.

Classroom settings

Open them with Play or with Change settings on the mission screen.

  • No predators: relaxed exploring, best for younger students.
  • Short game: 4 generations instead of 8. It applies when a new population starts.
  • Predictions: on by default. Turn it off to skip the guess and the Think like a scientist box.
  • Big text: larger words in menus and cards.

What is real and what is made up

River otters, the traits in the game, and the processes of natural selection, inheritance, mutation, and genetic drift are real. The ideas on the science cards, such as Bergmann's and Allen's rules and the links between habitat and the inner ear, are hypotheses that our lab and others are testing. The numbers are not real. Habitat targets, trait values, survival odds, and the brain and inner ear drawings are made up for play, and real populations take many more generations to change. The full rules, with every number, are on the How Otter Evolution works page.

Tell students: "The game is a model. Real scientists measure real otter skulls, brains, and inner ears to find out what really happened."
